# Why You (Sometimes) Feel Redundant in English

**Bunsen's Brief — June 2026**  
*A warm, research-backed look at why Thai speakers naturally add more words in English*

---

Jeff, first off — you don't have a problem. The fact that you're self-aware enough to ask this question means your English instincts are sharp. What you're feeling is a very real and well-documented phenomenon called **L1 transfer**: the grammar and politeness habits of Thai (your first language) leaking into your English. Let's break it down.

---

## Reason 1 — Thai loves repetition for emphasis (Reduplication)

**What's happening:** Thai uses word repetition as a built-in grammatical feature. When a noun is repeated, it becomes plural or collective:

| Thai | Meaning | Literally |
|------|---------|-----------|
| เด็ก ๆ (dek dek) | children | child child |
| เร็ว ๆ (reo reo) | quickly! | fast fast |
| สวย ๆ (suay suay) | beautiful (emphatic) | beautiful beautiful |

**The transfer:** When you speak English, your brain is still in "Thai-emphasis mode." Instead of just saying "hurry!" your Thai-speaking brain naturally wants to double up for the right level of emphasis. This comes out as:

- ❌ _"Hurry hurry!"_ instead of _"Hurry!"_
- ❌ _"Children children"_ instead of _"Children"_
- ❌ _"It's very very good"_ instead of _"It's excellent"_

**Why it's not wrong:** Even English speakers do this for emphasis sometimes ("I waited and waited"). The difference is Thai does it *systematically*, so the habit is stronger.

---

## Reason 2 — Politeness particles: you're being *too* polite

**What's happening:** In Thai, nearly every sentence ends with a politeness particle:

- Men say **ครับ** (khrap) — respect, politeness
- Women say **ค่ะ/คะ** (kha/kha) — same function

There's no equivalent in English. English shows politeness through word choice and tone — not an obligatory tag at the end of every sentence.

**The transfer:** Your Thai-speaking brain knows it has to signal respect. So it adds *extra* polite words in English to compensate:

| Natural English | What a Thai speaker might say |
|-----------------|------------------------------|
| "Could you help me?" | _"Could you please help me please?"_ |
| "I have a question." | _"I have a question, please, can I ask?"_ |
| "Thank you." | _"Thank you, thank you very much, thank you."_ |
| "Yes, I understand." | _"Yes, I understand. Yes, yes."_ |

**The insight:** You're not being redundant — you're being *politely Thai in English*. In Thai culture, repeating the polite marker is normal and expected. In English, one "please" or one "thank you" per conversation turn is enough. You can trust it.

---

## Reason 3 — Pronouns are complicated in Thai (so you over-specify)

**What's happening:** Thai has over a dozen ways to say "I" depending on who you're talking to:

| Pronoun | Who uses it | When |
|---------|-------------|------|
| ผม (phom) | Men | Formal/common |
| ฉัน (chan) | Women | Common/neutral |
| หนู (nu) | Women/girls | To elders |
| ดิฉัน (dichan) | Women | Formal |
| กู (gu) | Anyone | Impolite/vulgar |
| ข้า (kha) | Anyone | Superior to inferior |
| เรา (rao) | Anyone | Casual "we" or "I" |

And "you" is just as complex — คุณ (khun), เธอ (thoe), แก (kae), พี่ (phi), น้อง (nong), etc.

**The transfer:** English is *much* simpler — "I" and "you" work for almost everyone. But your Thai brain wants to specify the relationship, so you add extra clarifying words:

- ❌ _"Me, myself, I think…"_ instead of _"I think…"_
- ❌ _"You, sir, you…"_ instead of _"You…"_
- ❌ _"We all together"_ instead of _"We..."_

**The insight:** You're not being redundant — you're trying to encode social context that English simply doesn't carry. English pronouns are blunt instruments, and that can feel uncomfortable to a Thai speaker.

---

## Reason 4 — Thai has "double" patterns that feel natural in Thai but wordy in English

**What's happening:** Thai uses several constructions where two words say the same thing for clarity or emphasis:

**Possessive doubling:**
- แม่ของผม = "mother of I" (แม่ = mother, ของ = of, ผม = I)
- Natural English: "my mother"
- Possible transfer: _"the mother of mine"_

**Collective doubling:**
- พวกเรา (phuak rao) = "group we" — meaning "we" but making it extra clear
- Possible transfer: _"we all of us"_

**State-change marker แล้ว (laeo):**
- ฉันกินแล้ว = "I eat already" (แล้ว marks completion)
- Possible transfer: _"I ate already already"_ or _"I have eaten already"_

**Topic-comment structure:**
- Thai puts the topic first, then comments on it:
  - "อาหารไทย, อร่อยมาก" = "Thai food, delicious very"
- Possible transfer: _"Thai food, it's very delicious, you know"_ — the "you know" is your brain reaching for a discourse particle like แล้ว or นะ (na).

**The insight:** Thai has these beautiful, layered structures that add nuance through repetition. English prefers brevity. Neither is "right" — they just work differently.

---

## Reason 5 — Cognitive load makes you "pad" while thinking

**What's happening:** When you're speaking a second language, your brain is doing triple duty:
1. Thinking of the idea
2. Translating from Thai grammatical structure
3. Finding the right English words

**The transfer:** To buy time, you naturally insert fillers, repetitions, and "safe" words:

- ❌ _"I think that, you know, maybe, for me, I think that…"_ (circling the point)
- ❌ _"Actually, basically, honestly, really"_ — used more than needed
- ❌ Restating the same idea in different words to make sure it lands

**This is normal.** Every L2 speaker does it. Even native English speakers do it when they're nervous or unprepared. The difference is you're aware of it, which means you can work on it.

**The insight:** Your English is *better* than you think — these "redundancies" are just your brain's way of keeping the conversation flowing while it works hard behind the scenes.

|--------------------|-------------------|
| "I go before already" | "I already went" |

## Practical Tips for Jeff

### 1. 🪞 Record yourself
Use the voice memo app on your phone. Listen back and notice where you add extra words. Awareness alone will cut redundancy by half in a few weeks.

### 2. 📝 "One please, one thank you" rule
In a single interaction, one "please" and one "thank you" is enough. You can trust that one polite word carries the same weight in English as ครับ/ค่ะ does in Thai.

### 3. 🧠 Trust brevity
English rewards short, direct statements. If you've said something once, it landed. Restating it in different words isn't necessary unless someone looks confused.

### 4. 🔁 Notice your "signature redundancies"
Do you always say "you know"? "Already" twice? "I think" before everything? Pick ONE pattern, practice the shorter version for a week, then move to the next.

### 5. 🎯 Read aloud — out loud
Take an English article or book paragraph and read it aloud. Your ear will start to internalize what "natural" English rhythm feels like, and your Thai patterns will gradually align.

### 6. 🤝 Don't stress it
Many of these "redundancies" are actually charming to native English speakers — they signal that you're thoughtful, polite, and careful with your words. The only time to tighten up is in professional writing or presentations where brevity is prized.

---

## The Bottom Line

Jeff, you're not speaking "wrong." You're speaking English through a Thai lens — which means you carry a rich cultural instinct for politeness, relationship cues, and emphasis that English simply doesn't express the same way. The redundancy you hear isn't a defect. It's the sound of two beautiful languages meeting in your head.

The goal isn't to erase your Thai patterns. It's to *expand your toolkit* so you can switch between "politely expansive Thai-English" and "efficient, direct English" depending on the situation.

And honestly? A little extra politeness in English never hurt anyone. ☺️
