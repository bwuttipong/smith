# Why Thai Speakers Sound "Redundant" in English — A Brief for Jeff

**From:** Bunsen, Senior Research Lead
**For:** Jeff (Thai native speaker)
**Date:** June 2026

---

Jeff, you asked why you sometimes feel like you speak redundant or repetitive English. Great question — and the short answer is:

**It's not a flaw. It's your Thai brain doing its job perfectly, but speaking through an English mouth.**

Thai and English are structurally very different languages. Your brain is running Thai patterns under the hood, and those patterns happen to produce extra words when translated into English. Below are the four main reasons, with concrete examples and practical fixes.

---

## 1. Thai uses repetition as grammar (reduplication is built-in)

**What's happening:** In Thai, repeating a word **is** a grammatical operation. It turns singular into plural, adds emphasis, or softens meaning.

| Thai | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| เด็ก (dek) = child | เด็ก ๆ (dek dek) = children / group of children |
| เร็ว (reo) = fast | เร็ว ๆ (reo reo) = quickly! / hurry up! |
| สวย (suay) = beautiful | สวย ๆ (suay suay) = very beautiful / beautifully |

In English, repetition is mostly just repetition. But your Thai wiring treats it as a meaningful move — so you naturally reach for phrases like "quick quick," "beautiful beautiful," "walk walk" without realising it sounds redundant to native English ears.

**Example of carryover:**
> "I go walk walk at the park."
> "Let's do it fast fast."
> "The food is spicy spicy."

**The fix:** In English, adverbs and adjectives don't repeat for emphasis. Instead:
- "Walk walk" → "I went for a walk" or "I walked around."
- "Fast fast" → "Really fast" or "super quick."
- "Spicy spicy" → "Very spicy" or "really spicy."

---

## 2. Classifier thinking — Thai counts things differently

**What's happening:** In Thai, you ALWAYS use a classifier (a "category word") with numbers:

> ครู ห้า คน → teacher **five person**
> แก้ว สอง ใบ → glass **two leaf** (classifier for round objects)

English doesn't do this. We just say "five teachers" and "two glasses." But your brain wants to insert that extra category word because Thai grammar requires it. This carries over in subtle ways.

**Example of carryover:**
> "I have three **books** books." (your brain wants the classifier slot filled)
> "She has two **children** kids."
> "I need five **dollars** money."

More subtly, you might awkwardly feel you need to "explain the category" before the item:
> "In terms of food, I like spicy food" — instead of just "I like spicy food."

**The fix:** In English, just say [number] + [noun]. No helper word needed.
- ❌ "Three books books" → ✅ "Three books."
- ❌ "Two children kids" → ✅ "Two kids."
- ❌ "In terms of driving, I prefer..." → ✅ "I prefer driving..."

---

## 3. Politeness markers — your brain wants to soften every sentence

**What's happening:** This is the **biggest one** for Thai speakers. Thai has a rich system of politeness particles that tag onto the end of almost every sentence:

| Particle | Function |
|----------|----------|
| ครับ (kráp) | Polite marker (male speaker) |
| ค่ะ / คะ (khâ / khá) | Polite marker (female speaker) |
| นะ (ná) | Softener — makes requests friendly |
| จ้ะ / จ้า (jâ/jâa) | Emphasis, familiar softener |

In English, there's no equivalent to tacking "kráp" onto everything. So what does a Thai speaker do? They reach for English phrases that *feel* like they do the same job — and they pile them on:

> "I think maybe perhaps we could possibly… if that's okay with you, na?"

**Common redundant politeness patterns:**
| Thai impulse | English result |
|---|---|
| Wants to add kráp → | Adds "actually," "basically," "you know" to every sentence |
| Wants to add ná → | Adds "okay?" / "right?" / "nah" at the end |
| Wants to soften a request → | Triple-stacks hedging: "Could you possibly maybe help me a little bit if you don't mind?" |
| Wants to show respect → | Uses "I think," "maybe," "perhaps" even when certain |

**Example of carryover:**
> "I think maybe I will go to the shop, perhaps, if that's okay, you know?"
> "Actually, basically, the problem is difficult."

**The fix:** Pick **one** softener, not three.
- ❌ "I think perhaps maybe we could..." → ✅ "Maybe we could..."
- ❌ "Actually, basically, honestly, the thing is..." → ✅ "The thing is..."
- ❌ "Could you possibly maybe help a little bit, if it's okay?" → ✅ "Could you help me?"

**Permission to be direct:** In English, direct ≠ rude. "Can you help me?" is polite enough without "if that's okay" tacked on. Trust that English politeness lives in *tone and please/thank you* — not in piling on hedge words.

---

## 4. Serial verbs and time markers — explaining time from every angle

**What's happening:** Thai verbs don't change form for tense. Instead, time is marked with helper words:

| Thai | Role |
|------|------|
| กำลัง (gamlang) | "right now" — in progress |
| จะ (jà) | "will" — future |
| แล้ว (láew) | "already" — completed |
| เคย (koey) | "ever" — past experience |

These are optional in Thai — context tells you the time. But when speaking English, your brain knows English *needs* tense marking — so it **overcorrects** by layering multiple time markers to be absolutely sure the time is clear.

**Example of carryover:**
> "I will go tomorrow in the future."
> "I already finished it already."
> "Yesterday I was going to go but I didn't go, you know, before."
> "I have ever been to Japan before already."

**The fix:** In English, one time marker per sentence is enough.
- ❌ "I already finished already" → ✅ "I already finished" or "I finished."
- ❌ "I will go in the future tomorrow" → ✅ "I'll go tomorrow."
- ❌ "I have ever been before already" → ✅ "I've been there before."

English tense is shown by *verb form*, not by piling on time helpers. If you say "I went," the past is already clear.

## The cognitive load angle — why it gets worse when you're tired

When you're tired, stressed, or speaking fast, **all four patterns amplify**. Here's why:

- **Working memory is busy.** You're already spending mental energy on vocabulary, pronunciation, and sentence structure. The extra politeness hedges and repetitive patterns are "default" circuits your brain falls back on when the conscious processor is overloaded.
- **Monitoring drops.** Normally you catch yourself saying "walk walk" and correct it. When you're tired, that internal editor goes offline.
- **L1 takeover.** Under stress, your brain defaults to Thai sentence architecture and fills in English words. That produces more literal translations with redundant patterns.

This is completely normal. Every bilingual person experiences this. The patterns are more visible for Thai→English because the two languages differ in so many fundamental ways.

## Quick Reference Card

| Pattern | Thai origin | English fix |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Word repetition ("walk walk") | Reduplication is grammar | Use "very" or "really" instead |
| Extra category words ("books books") | Classifier system | Just [number] + [noun] |
| Piled-on hedges ("maybe perhaps") | Politeness particles (ครับ/นะ) | Pick ONE softener, trust directness |
| Double time markers ("already finished already") | Serial verb tenses | One time marker per sentence |

## Your personal action plan

1. **Pick one pattern to watch this week.** I'd suggest starting with #3 (politeness hedges) — it's the most common one and fixing it makes you sound significantly more confident.

2. **Record yourself for 2 minutes.** Speak about anything — your weekend, a problem at work, anything. Then listen back and count how many "maybe," "perhaps," "basically," "actually," "you know," and "like" you used. Just noticing is 80% of the fix.

3. **Tired? Slow down.** When you notice yourself getting wordy, take a breath. English rewards *fewer words with more precision* more than it rewards piling on softeners.

4. **Read it back mentally.** Before speaking a sentence that starts with "I think maybe I would possibly..." — pause and say it straight: "I'd like to..."

5. **Remember: direct ≠ rude.** In Thai culture, indirectness = politeness. In English, clarity + a smile = politeness. Different tools for the same job. You don't need to change *who you are* — just which tool you're using.

---

*Bottom line: What feels like "redundant English" is really "Thai doing its job in an English environment." The more you understand each pattern, the more control you have over it — on your terms, at your pace. You're not speaking badly. You're translating a beautiful language system in real time, and now you have the map.*
